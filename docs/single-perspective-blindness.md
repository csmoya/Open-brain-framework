# Single-Perspective Blindness: Evaluating Your Own Work

## The Problem

You wrote a strategy document. You've read it five times over two days. You've revised the structure, sharpened the language, addressed the objections you thought of. It feels complete.

What you've actually done is read it five times from the same perspective: yours. You can't unsee your own assumptions. The strategic leap you made in section two feels obvious to you — because you know the reasoning that led to it. A reader who doesn't share that reasoning will find it unsupported. The dependency you left implicit because it seemed self-evident is not self-evident to a VP who brings different priors. The evidence gap you glossed over because you find it convincing will stop a skeptic cold.

Single-perspective blindness is the reason most work that feels complete is missing something important. The problem isn't that you're not smart enough to catch the issues. The problem is structural: you cannot simultaneously be the author and the naive reader. You know too much about what you intended to say to notice where you didn't actually say it.

This applies beyond strategy documents. A piece of writing, a product brief, a plan, a decision memo — anything that will be read and evaluated by people with different expertise, different priors, and different incentives is vulnerable to single-perspective blindness.

---

## Why It Happens

### No fresh eyes mechanism

Getting genuine fresh-eyes feedback requires someone who doesn't already know what you meant to say. The closer someone is to your work and thinking, the less valuable their review — not because they're less smart, but because they share more of your assumptions.

Most practitioners don't have a systematic way to get genuinely fresh perspectives. They ask colleagues (who share context), they ask friends (who are too polite to be harsh), or they sit on the work for a few days and re-read it themselves (which helps with proofreading but doesn't help with structural blind spots, because your assumptions haven't changed).

### Lack of domain expertise in evaluators

The ideal reviewer for a strategy document is simultaneously a rigorous thinker about communication structure, a domain expert who can evaluate the claims, and a skeptic who will specifically look for the strongest objection to your position. No individual person combines all three at high fidelity.

When you ask a single reviewer — even a very good one — you're getting coverage from one angle. The communication expert might miss the strategic gap. The domain expert might not flag the ambiguous pronoun on page two. The colleague who agrees with your conclusion will find the argument convincing even where it's thin, because they're filling gaps with their own knowledge.

Multi-perspective review requires multiple perspectives, not multiple readers who share the same perspective.

### Feedback timing

Most review happens after a draft is complete. At that point, the author has psychological investment in the artifact. Structural feedback — "the second section doesn't logically follow from the first" — feels threatening rather than useful, because it implies significant rework.

This creates a subtle dynamic where reviewers pull their punches on structural criticism and focus on surface-level feedback ("maybe add more examples in section three"). The author feels reviewed; the structural problems remain.

---

## How to Detect It

**The gap between anxiety and feedback:** When you share work, do you have a specific worry about a particular weakness — a logical jump, a dependency you haven't defended, an assumption that might not hold? Does the feedback you receive address that specific thing?

If the answer is "I was worried about X, but nobody mentioned X" — that's the blind spot. Not because the reviewers are incompetent, but because they're not reading from the same angle as the person who would be most likely to catch X.

**The objection test:** After receiving feedback, can you still articulate the three strongest arguments against your position? If the feedback didn't surface any new objections — if you already knew everything that was pointed out — you got editorial review, not critical review.

**The perspective count:** How many distinct expert lenses reviewed this work? One reviewer brings one perspective. Even a thoughtful, senior reader only covers certain angles. If the answer is "one person read it," you have single-perspective blindness by design.

---

## How to Fix It

**Multi-perspective review by design**

Instead of asking for "feedback," define the specific expert lenses the work needs reviewed from. For a strategy document:

| Lens | Focus | What they'll find |
|---|---|---|
| Communication expert | Structure, clarity, flow | Where the argument breaks down, where claims are unsupported |
| Domain expert | Accuracy, completeness, strategic coherence | What's missing, what's wrong, what contradicts established knowledge |
| Skeptic / Devil's advocate | The strongest objection to your position | Where you've glossed over a hard problem, where the evidence is thin |

All three should review the same work, and their feedback should be synthesized. When two lenses agree on a problem, the problem is real. When they disagree, you have a genuine tension to resolve.

**In-character expert simulation**

Generic reviews produce generic feedback ("good job," "needs more examples"). In-character reviews produce specific feedback from a known perspective.

Instead of "what would an expert in product strategy think of this?" use "what would someone who wrote a book arguing that strategy without execution is worthless specifically object to in section two?" The in-character constraint forces specificity. The review becomes: "This person's primary lens would be X. Reading from that lens, the first thing they'd notice is..."

This approach scales poorly if you need to find real people with each expertise. It scales well if the perspectives can be simulated from documented frameworks and known expert positions.

**The antagonist review**

For any document that takes a position, assign one explicit review role: find the strongest argument against the position. Not minor quibbles, not copy edits — the most compelling objection a thoughtful opponent would raise.

This review should be done by someone who genuinely tries to find the best version of the counterargument, not someone who gently notes that "there might be some pushback." The best counterargument is the one that, if unanswered, would cause a reader to reject your position. Finding it before publication is strictly better than encountering it after.
