# Knowledge-Grounded Output: Writing and Thinking from Your Own Evidence

## What It Is

Knowledge-Grounded Output is a structured approach to turning a knowledge vault into published work. It treats the path from idea to artifact as a pipeline with five distinct phases, each with a specific purpose and a specific failure mode it prevents.

The name reflects the core premise: knowledge that never leaves the vault doesn't fulfill the purpose of building it. Capture, synthesis, and retrieval are necessary but not sufficient. The output phase — turning durable knowledge into arguments, posts, documents, strategies, and decisions — is where the vault justifies its cost.

The five phases address the most common reasons knowledge vaults fail to produce output: starting with a topic instead of a claim, skipping retrieval before writing, treating the blank document as the starting point, evaluating work from a single perspective, and learning new material without activating what's already known.

---

## The Five Phases

### Phase 1: Claim-First Writing

**Purpose:** Establish the specific, falsifiable position that organizes everything that follows.

A claim is not a topic. A topic is an area ("product discovery"). A claim is a specific position that a smart, informed person could reasonably disagree with: "Most product teams do discovery at the wrong stage — after problem framing, not before."

The claim test: if no reasonable person would disagree, you have a topic, not a claim. Keep narrowing until the position has an opponent.

The claim serves as an organizing principle for retrieval, drafting, and review. Every note you retrieve, every sentence you write, every objection you address is in service of defending or refining this specific position.

**Common mistake:** Mistaking a specific topic for a claim. "The difference between qualitative and quantitative research" is a topic. "Qualitative research produces better product decisions than quantitative research in early-stage development" is a claim.

### Phase 2: Vault-Grounded Evidence

**Purpose:** Retrieve the specific evidence in your vault that bears on the claim — before writing a single sentence of draft.

After writing the claim, query your vault explicitly for notes that:
- Support the claim with evidence or principle
- Contradict the claim in ways you'll need to address or refine
- Nuance the claim by adding conditions, exceptions, or boundaries

Do not browse generally. Query specifically, with the claim as the search anchor. Retrieve 5-15 notes. Organize them by their relationship to the claim.

This phase ensures the draft is written from your actual knowledge base, not from the general impressions that happen to be in working memory. The notes become the evidence structure; the draft becomes the argument built from that evidence.

**Common mistake:** Treating this phase as optional or running it after drafting. Running retrieval after writing produces a draft you're trying to support, not a draft you built from evidence. The sequence matters.

### Phase 3: Conversational Draft

**Purpose:** Convert the claim and evidence structure into a draft through conversation, not through typing into a blank document.

The blank document is a high-friction writing environment. It requires you to produce polished sentences while simultaneously holding your argument structure, managing evidence, and staying on the claim. This combination is cognitively expensive and slows most writers significantly.

The conversational draft replaces the blank document with a conversation. You articulate the claim. You explain why someone would disagree. You defend the claim using the evidence you retrieved. You answer hard questions. You refine the position as objections expose weak spots.

The conversation produces most of the sentences you'll need. The question-and-answer structure maps naturally to argument structure. A 15-25 minute conversation on a well-prepared claim typically yields enough material for a complete draft.

**Common mistake:** Using the conversational draft as a brainstorming session without a prior claim. The conversation only produces a draft if the claim is clear at the start. Without a claim, the conversation produces exploration — which is valuable but is not a draft.

### Phase 4: Multi-Perspective Review

**Purpose:** Evaluate the draft through expert lenses that you cannot apply to your own work.

Before publishing or sharing a draft with stakeholders, run it through at least three distinct review lenses simultaneously:

| Lens | Focus | Typical output |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Structure, clarity, argument flow, unsupported leaps | Structural problems, ambiguities, missing transitions |
| Domain | Accuracy, completeness, consistency with field knowledge | Missing evidence, wrong claims, strategic gaps |
| Skeptic | The strongest objection to the position | The most compelling counterargument the draft doesn't address |

The three lenses should review in parallel, not sequentially. Synthesize their feedback: where two or more lenses flag the same issue, that issue is confirmed. Where a lens flags something the others don't, evaluate whether it reflects a real gap or a perspective mismatch.

**Common mistake:** Treating this as "asking someone to proofread." Proofreading is a subset of the communication lens, and it's the least important part. The domain and skeptic lenses are where the substance is. A draft without a skeptic review has a known blind spot: the strongest objection has never been formally tested.

### Phase 5: Prior Knowledge Activation

**Purpose:** Before any significant learning session on a topic, retrieve what you already know about it — so you can notice where new knowledge contradicts, extends, or refines the old.

This phase sits before the learning cycle, not the output cycle. It prevents a common failure: learning new material on a topic where you have prior vault notes, but starting from scratch because you didn't check what you already knew.

Starting from zero reduces retention (you can't anchor new knowledge to existing structure), reduces transfer (you don't notice when new knowledge applies to old problems), and misses contradictions (the new source says X; your vault says not-X — but you won't notice unless both are active at the same time).

**Common mistake:** Running this phase only for unfamiliar topics. Prior knowledge activation is most valuable precisely on topics where you think you already know the material — because that's where the most significant refinements and contradictions hide.

## The Process

| Phase | Input | Output | Failure mode prevented |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim-First Writing | Topic or idea | Specific, falsifiable position | Writing from topics instead of positions |
| Vault-Grounded Evidence | Claim | Organized evidence from vault notes | Writing from impressions instead of evidence |
| Conversational Draft | Claim + evidence structure | Complete draft material | Blank page paralysis; high-friction writing |
| Multi-Perspective Review | Draft | Identified gaps, structural problems, best counterargument | Single-perspective blindness |
| Prior Knowledge Activation | Learning topic | Relevant prior notes surfaced and active | Starting from zero; missing contradictions and extensions |

---

## Cadence

Knowledge-Grounded Output is not a writing system — it's the operational connection between knowledge management and knowledge use. It runs whenever you need to produce something from your vault.

- **For significant pieces** (strategy documents, substantial posts, decision memos): run all five phases in sequence. Total time: 45-90 minutes for a well-maintained vault.
- **For routine pieces** (shorter posts, quick takes, meeting prep): run Phases 1, 2, and 3 at minimum. Phases 4 and 5 scale to the stakes.
- **For learning sessions:** Phase 5 (prior knowledge activation) should run at the start of every substantial learning session, regardless of whether output is the goal.

---

## What It Is Not

**Not AI-generated content.** The conversational draft uses dialogue to lower writing friction; it doesn't replace the author's position, evidence, or judgment. The claim must be yours. The evidence must come from your vault. The draft is yours to accept, reject, or rewrite.

**Not template-filling.** Running through the phases doesn't produce output automatically. Phase 2 requires judgment about which notes are actually relevant. Phase 3 requires defending a position under challenge. Phase 4 requires deciding which feedback to address. The system structures the work; it doesn't do the work.

**Not productivity theater.** The phases are not optimized for feeling productive — they're optimized for producing knowledge-grounded output. Some phases are slow and uncomfortable (developing a claim you can defend, facing the best counterargument to your position). The discomfort is the mechanism, not a sign of poor design.

**Not a replacement for the vault.** This system is the output layer on top of a functioning knowledge vault. It requires notes, it requires synthesis, it requires a graph with semantic connections. Without those, Phase 2 has nothing to retrieve and the whole pipeline collapses to ordinary writing.

## Measuring Impact

| Metric | Before | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Time from idea to complete draft | Days / weeks | 45-90 minutes for significant pieces |
| Vault contribution per piece | "I found a quote" | 3+ specific notes directly informing argument |
| Claims traceable to vault notes | < 30% | > 70% |
| Critical feedback received before publishing | None or vague | At least one structural issue found and addressed per piece |
| Output volume | Self-report | Increasing trend over 90 days |

The single most reliable indicator that the system is working: someone reads your work and asks "where did you get that?" — and you can trace the claim directly to a note you built before you started writing.
